## 1a. Analysis - what the preparation numbers mean

### The metrics used on this page

**Class share (%)** is the fraction of all flows belonging to one class. It
matters because a classifier that always predicts the largest class already
scores that share as its accuracy - here
39.3% - without learning anything.

**Imbalance ratio** is the size of the largest class divided by the size of the
smallest. Measured value: **414 : 1**. For every
ICMPFlood flow in the data there are roughly
414 Benign flows. A model trained
to minimise average error is therefore rewarded for ignoring
ICMPFlood entirely, which is the specific failure
Objective 1 sets out to expose and Objective 4 sets out to fix.

**Missing rate** is the fraction of flows for which a column has no value. In
network-flow data this is a signal, not noise - the absence of a reverse-
direction statistic means there was no reverse-direction traffic.

**Accuracy ceiling (Bayes-optimal accuracy)** is the best score obtainable by
*any* classifier on a given feature representation. It is computed exactly
here: flows with byte-for-byte identical feature vectors cannot be separated by
any function of those features, so the optimum is to predict the majority label
inside each identical-feature group. The accuracy of that rule is the ceiling.

### What the measured values indicate

**The imbalance is severe and real.** At 414:1 the dataset
sits well outside the range where plain accuracy is meaningful. This confirms
research gap 3 from the proposal quantitatively, on the data itself, and it
justifies the per-class recall reporting used throughout the remaining
objectives.

**Two columns in the published CSV leak the label.** This is the most
consequential finding of the preparation stage. `Seq` and `Offset` are
artefacts of how the dataset file was assembled. On their own, with no
information about packets, bytes, protocols or timing, they reach
**96.78%** binary accuracy. Including them lifts a
standard XGBoost model from **76.60%** to
**99.98%** - a gap of
23.4% that measures file layout rather
than intrusion detection. Any published figure near 99.9% on this CSV needs to
state whether these columns were excluded; where a study does not say, its
headline number is not directly comparable with the ones reported here. This
project removes them, so every result that follows is a genuine
traffic-based detection result.

**The honest ceiling is 76.85%, not 100%.**
52.0% of flows share an identical
feature vector with a flow carrying a different label. The dominant collision
is between benign traffic and UDPFlood:
a single-packet benign UDP exchange and a single-packet UDP flood packet
produce the same Argus flow record. No model, glass-box or black-box, can
separate them from these features. This reframes every accuracy number in the
project: a detector scoring 76.35% is not a
mediocre detector, it is a detector operating at the limit of what the data
allows.

**Interpreting the figures.** In the class-distribution figure the bar lengths
span three orders of magnitude - the visual gap between the top and bottom bars
*is* the imbalance problem. In the leakage-audit figure, the middle bar is the
one to read: it uses no traffic information at all, yet nearly matches the full
model. In the collision figure, the two tall bars identify precisely which
classes the ceiling comes from.

### How this supports Research Objective 1

Objective 1 asks for the dataset to be prepared and profiled, with the
minority-class picture made explicit. This stage delivers that and adds two
pieces of due diligence the objective implies but does not spell out:

1. The data is cleaned, encoded and split once, into a fixed stratified
   60/20/20 partition that every subsequent model reuses. This is what makes
   the glass-box versus black-box comparison in Objectives 2 and 3 a
   like-for-like comparison rather than an approximate one.
2. The feature set is audited before any model is trusted. Removing `Seq` and
   `Offset` and establishing the 76.85% ceiling
   means the accuracy comparisons that follow are measuring detection skill,
   and are being judged against an attainable target.

The remaining half of Objective 1 - baseline detectors and their rare-class
detection rates - is covered in the next section.

## 1b. Analysis - what the baseline numbers mean

### The metrics used on this page

**Accuracy** is the fraction of test flows classified correctly. It is the
figure normally quoted in the 5G-NIDD literature, and it is dominated by the
largest classes: the two biggest classes here cover roughly
77%
of the data between them, so a model can score highly while never detecting a
single ICMPFlood.

**Recall** (also called the detection rate) is, for one class, the share of
that class's flows the model actually caught: recall = true positives /
(true positives + false negatives). If a model scored a recall of 0.20 on
ICMPFlood, four out of every five ICMPFlood attacks would pass through
undetected. This is the metric an operator cares about for a specific attack
type, and the measured values for every class are in the table above.

**Precision** is the share of the model's alerts for a class that were correct.
Recall and precision trade off; F1 is their harmonic mean.

**Macro-F1** averages the per-class F1 scores giving every class equal weight,
regardless of size. Unlike accuracy it cannot be inflated by the majority
class, which makes it the honest single-number summary under this dataset's
severe imbalance.

**Balanced accuracy** is the mean of the per-class recalls - the same idea
applied to recall alone.

**Rare-class recall** is the unweighted mean recall over
ICMPFlood, SYNFlood, UDPScan, SYNScan, TCPConnectScan, computed here so the minority picture is a
number rather than an impression.

**MCC** (Matthews correlation coefficient) summarises the whole confusion
matrix on a -1 to +1 scale and stays meaningful under imbalance.

**False-alarm rate** is the share of genuinely benign flows the model labels as
an attack (one minus benign recall). It is the metric that determines whether
analysts trust a detector day to day.

### What the measured values indicate

**Binary accuracy clusters tightly around
0.758.** All four baselines land within
0.033 of one
another. When four models of very different capacity - a depth-limited tree, a
linear model, an instance-based method and a 150-tree ensemble - all agree to
three decimal places, the limit being hit is the *data*, not the model. That is
exactly what the accuracy ceiling computed in section 1a predicted.

**The 9-class results tell a different story from the binary ones.** Overall
9-class accuracy stays near the binary figure, but macro-F1 spreads from
0.8990 to 0.9288.
That spread is entirely about the rare classes: accuracy cannot see them,
macro-F1 can.

**The minority-class picture.** **The gap runs in the opposite direction to the one
usually assumed, and this is a substantive finding.** Mean recall on the rare
classes is 0.9920; mean recall on the
common classes is only 0.8292. Every
baseline detects ICMPFlood, SYNFlood and the three scan types at or near
perfect recall despite their tiny share of the data.

The reason is separability rather than sample size. A rare class is only hard
to learn when it is *also* hard to distinguish. ICMPFlood and the port scans
carry unmistakable flow signatures - a single-protocol, fixed-size, fixed-rate
pattern that no benign traffic imitates - so even a handful of training
examples pins them down. Imbalance alone did not defeat these baselines on this
dataset.

What overall accuracy actually hides here is the **false-alarm rate**.
LogisticRegression misclassifies 59.3% of benign traffic as an
attack while still reporting
75.8% overall accuracy. In a security
operations centre that is the difference between a usable detector and one the
analysts switch off. The per-class table makes it visible; the headline number
does not.

**Interpreting the figures.** Read the per-class recall heat map row by row:
each row is one model, and the pale cells mark the classes it cannot handle.
The pale column is **Benign** for every model, while the rare
attack columns stay dark. In the gap figure, the first bar of each group is the
number a paper would report; the second, third and fourth bars are the three
different realities it averages over.

### How this supports Research Objective 1

Objective 1 asks for baseline detectors that report "both overall accuracy and
the detection rate of the rare attack classes to expose the minority-class
gap". Every part of that is now delivered as measured evidence:

- Four baseline detectors, drawn from the classifier family used in the
  5G-NIDD literature, trained on the identical fixed split.
- Overall accuracy reported for both the binary and the 9-class task.
- Per-class recall reported for all 9 classes, with the rare
  classes flagged, exported to CSV and plotted.
- The gap itself quantified per model and per class, rather than left as a
  qualitative claim.

The objective asked for the minority-class gap to be *exposed* - measured
rather than assumed - and that is what the numbers above do. On this dataset
the measurement contradicts the expectation stated in the proposal's research
gap 3: rare-attack recall is not the weak point, benign-versus-UDPFlood
separation is. Reporting that honestly is the point of the exercise, and it
sharpens the target for the objectives that follow. Objective 2 must show the
glass-box detector matches these baselines on accuracy; Objective 4 tests
whether imbalance handling can move rare-class recall that is already near its
ceiling, and what it costs elsewhere.
