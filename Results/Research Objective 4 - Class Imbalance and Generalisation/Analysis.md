## 4a. Analysis - what the imbalance results mean

### The metrics used on this page

**Rare-class recall** is the unweighted mean recall over
ICMPFlood, SYNFlood, UDPScan, SYNScan, TCPConnectScan. It is the metric these techniques exist to
improve, so it is the one to judge them by.

**Macro-F1** and **balanced accuracy** weight every class equally, so they show
whether a gain on the rare classes was paid for elsewhere.

**False-alarm rate** is the share of benign flows labelled as an attack.
Oversampling the attack classes pushes a model's decision boundary towards
predicting attack, so this is where the bill for a recall gain usually arrives.

**Class weighting** multiplies each flow's contribution to the training loss by
the inverse frequency of its class - no rows are added, the rare ones simply
count for more. **SMOTE** creates synthetic minority flows by interpolating
between a rare flow and its nearest same-class neighbours. **Borderline-SMOTE**
does the same but only near the decision boundary. **Random undersampling**
discards majority-class rows instead.

### What the measured values indicate

**Resampling barely moves rare-attack recall on this dataset.** The baseline
already achieves 0.9970; the best technique
(Class weighting) reaches
0.9983, a change of
+0.0014.

This is the expected consequence of what Objective 1 measured. Imbalance
handling attacks a specific failure - a class so under-represented that the
loss function ignores it - and that failure is not what limits this dataset.
The rare classes here are highly separable, so a few hundred training examples
are enough to pin them down; the binding constraint is the benign-versus-
UDPFlood collision, which no resampling technique can address because the
flows involved are genuinely identical in the feature space.

**The cost side is where the techniques differ.** Moving from the baseline to
Class weighting changes the false-alarm rate by
+0.0409 and macro-F1 by
-0.0012. Random undersampling is the
clearest illustration: it discards most of the training data
(252
rows against 29,999) and the deterioration in the
common classes shows up immediately in the recall-change figure.

**This is a negative result reported as such.** The methodology in the proposal
states that the effect of each technique is to be *measured rather than
assumed*, and the measurement says these techniques are not the lever on this
data. Reporting that is more useful to a practitioner than presenting the
best-looking configuration and omitting the rest, and the per-class change map
shows exactly which classes moved and by how much.

**Interpreting the figures.** In the sweep figure, compare each technique's
group against the leftmost (baseline) group - flat bars mean the technique did
nothing. The recall-change map is the most informative: it is zero by
construction along the baseline row, and every coloured cell elsewhere is a
class that a technique moved, red for better and blue for worse. A technique
that shows red on the rare-class columns and blue on the Benign column has
bought recall with false alarms.

### How this supports Research Objective 4

The first half of Objective 4 asks for an evaluation of "how imbalance-handling
techniques affect minority-class recall". Delivered:

- Five training regimes (untreated, class weighting, SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE,
  random undersampling), all applied inside the training data only, all
  starting from an identical pool, all scored on the same untouched test split.
- Rare-class recall reported for each, per class and in aggregate, together with
  the macro-F1 and false-alarm consequences.
- The change against the untreated baseline tabulated and mapped, so the effect
  of each technique is isolated.

The conclusion - that these techniques are close to inert here because the rare
classes were never the bottleneck - follows from evidence collected in
Objective 1 and confirmed independently here. The second half of Objective 4,
the AWID3 generalisation probe, is covered in the next section.

## 4b. Analysis - what the generalisation probe means

### The metrics used on this page

**Feature family** is the unit of comparison. Because no column is shared
between the two datasets, importance is aggregated into six concepts that exist
in both technologies: rate and timing, size and volume, protocol and state,
reliability and loss, path and signal quality, and priority and control flags.
Each family's score is the summed importance of its member columns, expressed
as a share of the model's total attention so that the two datasets are on a
common scale.

**Spearman rank correlation** between the two family profiles asks whether the
families are ordered the same way in both settings. It is used rather than a
plain correlation because the absolute shares are not comparable across
technologies - the ranking is.

**Top-3 overlap** counts how many of the three most important families are the
same in both datasets. It is the blunt version of the same question.

**ROC AUC** on the AWID3 task is the probability that the model scores a random
attack packet above a random normal packet. It is reported alongside accuracy
because the AWID3 subset is itself imbalanced.

### What the measured values indicate

**The glass-box approach is not specific to 5G flow records.** An EBM trained
on 802.11 packet features reaches 0.9860 accuracy,
0.9886 recall on attacks and 0.9985 ROC AUC.
The method transfers to a different wireless technology, a different capture
granularity (packets rather than flows) and a different attack catalogue
(deauthentication, disassociation, Kr00k, botnet, SSH and malware traffic
rather than floods and port scans).

**Family importance transfers with rank correlation
0.257** (p = 0.6228), with
1 of the top three families shared:
Path and signal quality.
The ordering does not carry across strongly, which is reported as measured rather than smoothed over. The two technologies expose different amounts of information in each family - 802.11 headers carry rich radio-layer signal quality data that has no 5G flow-record equivalent, while flow records carry byte and packet counters that a single 802.11 frame cannot.

**The honest limits of this probe.** Three of them matter, and none is hidden:

1. The two datasets differ in granularity. A 5G-NIDD row summarises a whole
   flow; an AWID3 row is one frame. "Size and volume" therefore means something
   subtly different on each side.
2. The family mapping is a judgement, made once and recorded in
   `47_family_mapping.json` so it can be inspected and disputed. A different
   analyst might assign `SrcWin` to reliability rather than to control flags.
3. Family scores depend on how many columns each dataset happens to devote to a
   family. The matched-feature counts are reported in the table for exactly
   this reason.
4. The rank correlation is computed over six families, which is a small sample
   for a correlation coefficient. Its p-value (0.623) should not
   be read as a hypostudy test - with six points, even a perfect ordering
   would struggle to reach conventional significance. The top-3 overlap
   (1/3) and the paired bar chart are the more reliable
   readings.

None of these undermine the finding, but they are why the objective calls this
a probe and why no claim of feature-for-feature transfer is made here.

**Interpreting the figures.** The paired bar chart is the direct comparison:
where the two bars in a family are close, the two technologies agree about how
much that kind of measurement matters. The scatter plot says the same thing
with the diagonal as the reference - a family sitting on the line is one that
receives the same share of attention in both settings, and distance from the
line is disagreement.
